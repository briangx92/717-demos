How a concrete job goes.

Five steps, same order, every time. This is the trade itself — what has to happen between "we need concrete" and a slab you can park on.

01
Prep

Site prep & formwork

The job is won or lost before the truck shows up. The ground gets graded and compacted, a stone base goes down, and wooden forms are staked in dead true to the layout. Rebar or wire mesh gets tied in where the slab needs the muscle.

Why it matters: 90% of cracked concrete traces back to bad prep — soft ground, no base, or forms that wandered. This is the unglamorous part that decides whether your slab lasts decades or heaves next winter.
02
Pour

The pour

The mixer pulls up and the chute goes in the form. Concrete flows out while the crew rakes and shovels it into every corner — placed, not dumped. On a big slab this is a choreographed few hours; concrete waits for no one once it starts setting.

Good to know: weather calls the schedule. Pouring in a downpour or a hard freeze ruins the finish, so a good crew will move your date before they'll pour in bad conditions.
03
Level

Screed — strike it flat

While the concrete's still wet, a long screed board rides the tops of the forms and strikes off every high spot in one pass. What's left is a flat plane, edge to edge. This single step is the difference between a slab that drains right and one that ponds water forever.

Why it matters: you can't fix flatness later. Grinding a high slab costs more than pouring it did. The screed pass is where the crew earns its money.
04
Finish

Float, edge & finish

Bull floats knock down the texture, hand trowels work the surface, and the edges get tooled clean. This is where you pick your finish — broom for grip on a driveway, smooth for a garage floor, or a stamped pattern pressed in while it's still soft.

Good to know: the finish is the only part you'll ever see. Everything before it is structure; this pass is what your eyes and feet judge for the next thirty years.
05
Cure

Cut, cure & seal

Control joints get cut so the slab cracks where it's supposed to — in straight lines you barely notice, not spiderwebs across the middle. Then it cures, hardens, and usually gets sealed. Foot traffic in a day or two; vehicles after about a week.

Why it matters: concrete keeps getting stronger for weeks. The crew that tells you to stay off it a few extra days isn't being cautious — they're protecting the strength you paid for.
